# Exemplar Infographic – Design Description

An exemplar infographic feels clear, focused, and visually inviting at first glance. A viewer can quickly understand the main message, follow a smooth visual path, and recall key points afterward.

## Overall Structure and Layout

- **Clear focal point:**  
  The main title or central visual immediately tells the viewer what the infographic is about.
- **Logical sections:**  
  Content is grouped into clearly labeled sections (e.g., Introduction, Key Concepts, Evidence/Data, Implications, Conclusion). Each section is visually separated using spacing, subtle lines, or background shapes.
- **Consistent grid:**  
  Elements align to an invisible grid. Text boxes, icons, and charts line up cleanly, avoiding a “scattered” look.
- **Intentional white space:**  
  There is enough empty space between elements so nothing feels cramped. White space is used to separate ideas and guide the eye.
- **Smooth reading path:**  
  The layout leads the viewer through the information in a predictable order (usually top-to-bottom and left-to-right). Arrows, numbering, or visual cues show the sequence.

## Typography (Text Style)

- **Limited, consistent fonts:**  
  Uses 2–3 fonts at most:
  - One for headings,
  - One for body text,
  - Optional accent font for small labels or callouts.
- **Clear hierarchy:**  
  - Title: largest text on the page.  
  - Section headings: clearly smaller than the title but larger than body text.  
  - Body text: readable at a comfortable size, even when printed.
- **Readable formatting:**  
  - Sentence case or title case is used consistently.  
  - Line length is moderate (no very wide text blocks).  
  - Line spacing makes text easy to read.
- **Strong contrast:**  
  Text color contrasts clearly with the background (e.g., dark text on a light background). There are no important details in hard-to-read color combinations.

## Color Palette

- **Limited palette:**  
  Uses a small set of harmonious colors (e.g., 2–3 main colors plus neutrals).
- **Purposeful use:**  
  - Colors indicate categories, sections, or relationships.  
  - Important elements (titles, key numbers, main conclusions) are highlighted with accent colors.
- **Consistent application:**  
  The same color always means the same thing (e.g., one color for “benefits,” another for “risks”).
- **Accessible choices:**  
  High contrast is maintained; bright or saturated colors are used sparingly so they don’t overwhelm. Color is never the *only* cue for understanding something (labels, icons, or patterns also help).

## Imagery, Icons, and Graphics

- **Relevant visuals:**  
  Photos, illustrations, and icons directly support the content (e.g., diagrams of cells for a biotechnology topic, not generic clip art).
- **Consistent style:**  
  Icons and illustrations share a similar style (line weight, color treatment). No mix of highly realistic photos with random cartoon-style icons unless intentionally integrated.
- **High quality:**  
  Images are sharp, not pixelated or distorted. There are no stretched or squashed pictures.
- **Clear labeling:**  
  - All diagrams and images that convey information include labels or captions.  
  - Arrows or callouts show exactly what the viewer should notice.
- **Balanced quantity:**  
  There are enough visuals to support comprehension, but not so many that they crowd out the text or feel decorative without purpose.

## Data Visualization (If Data Is Included)

- **Appropriate chart types:**  
  The chart matches the type of data (e.g., bar charts for comparisons, line graphs for trends, simple pie charts for proportions).
- **Accurate and honest:**  
  - Axes are labeled and scaled correctly.  
  - There are no misleading truncations or distortions.
- **Readable details:**  
  - Tick marks, labels, and legends are large enough to read.  
  - Few data series per chart to avoid clutter.
- **Integrated explanations:**  
  Short explanations or captions interpret the data for the viewer (“This shows that…”).

## Text Content and Explanations

- **Concise and chunked:**  
  Information appears in short blocks, phrases, or bullet points—not long paragraphs.
- **Direct connections:**  
  Each text block is clearly tied to a nearby visual (image, icon, or chart).
- **Clarifying explanations:**  
  - Key terms are defined briefly.  
  - Each statistic or fact is followed by a short note explaining its significance.
- **Consistent voice and level:**  
  Language fits the audience (e.g., clear, student-friendly explanations for classmates).

## Storytelling and Flow

- **Clear beginning, middle, end:**
  - **Beginning:** Introduces the topic and its importance in one or two short sections.  
  - **Middle:** Presents main ideas, evidence, mechanisms, or comparisons in a logical order.  
  - **End:** Summarizes key takeaways and, if relevant, points to implications, applications, or actions.
- **Guiding devices:**  
  Arrows, numbered steps, or pathways visually guide the viewer through the narrative.

## Creativity and Thematic Design

- **Thoughtful theme:**  
  Visual theme reflects the topic (e.g., a DNA double helix motif in dividers for a genetics infographic) without overpowering readability.
- **Original touches:**  
  Custom icons, clever metaphors, or creative layouts help the viewer understand complex ideas more easily.
- **Balanced flair:**  
  Decorative elements support the message rather than distract from it.

## References and Attribution

- **Designated reference area:**  
  At the bottom or side, there is a clean section for sources.
- **Readable but unobtrusive:**  
  References are in smaller text so they don’t dominate, but they are still legible.
- **Consistent formatting:**  
  References follow a clear, recognizable format (e.g., APA), and image credits are included when appropriate.

# Poor Infographic – Design Description (Opposite of the Exemplar)

A poor infographic feels confusing, cluttered, or empty. The viewer does not immediately understand the main point and may not know where to look first.

## Overall Structure and Layout

- **No clear focal point:**  
  Title is small, lost, or missing; the viewer has to search to figure out what the infographic is about.
- **Random placement of elements:**  
  Text boxes, images, and charts are scattered without alignment. There is no logical grouping or sectioning of ideas.
- **Inconsistent spacing:**  
  Some areas are crammed with content while others have large blank gaps that don’t serve a purpose.
- **Chaotic reading path:**  
  The eye jumps around the page because there are no visual cues for order. Arrows, numbers, or headings are missing or confusing.

## Typography (Text Style)

- **Too many fonts or styles:**  
  Uses many different fonts and font weights, creating visual noise.
- **Poor hierarchy:**  
  Titles, headings, and body text are similar sizes; important information doesn’t stand out.
- **Tiny or overly dense text:**  
  Paragraphs are long, line spacing is tight, and text blocks are difficult to read. Some text may be so small it is almost illegible.
- **Low contrast:**  
  Text might be placed on busy backgrounds or in colors too similar to the background (e.g., light gray on white, red on dark blue), making it hard to distinguish.

## Color Palette

- **Overloaded palette:**  
  Uses many unrelated colors without a clear system. Colors clash or feel random.
- **No consistent meaning:**  
  The same color is used for unrelated things; different colors sometimes represent the same category.
- **Distracting saturation:**  
  Neon or overly bright colors dominate large areas, making it tiring to look at.
- **Poor accessibility:**  
  Important information is encoded only in color (e.g., “red means X, green means Y”) with no labels or patterns. Low contrast combinations make elements unreadable.

## Imagery, Icons, and Graphics

- **Irrelevant or generic visuals:**  
  Clip art or random images are included that do not specifically relate to the topic or the nearby text.
- **Mixed, inconsistent styles:**  
  High-detail photos sit next to cartoon icons and 3D graphics with no unifying look.
- **Low resolution or distorted images:**  
  Pictures appear pixelated, stretched, squashed, or cropped strangely.
- **Unlabeled visuals:**  
  Diagrams lack labels, and images lack captions. The viewer cannot tell what to pay attention to.
- **Visual overload or emptiness:**  
  Either too many visuals crowd the space and compete with each other, or there are very few visuals and large blocks of text dominate.

## Data Visualization Problems

- **Inappropriate chart choice:**  
  Complex data is displayed in confusing or misleading charts (e.g., a 3D pie chart with many tiny slices).
- **Missing labels and units:**  
  Axes, legends, and units of measurement are absent or unclear.
- **Misleading scales:**  
  Axes are truncated or irregular, exaggerating or hiding differences.
- **Tiny, unreadable details:**  
  Numbers, axis labels, and legends are too small or overlap with other elements.

## Text Content and Explanations

- **Walls of text:**  
  Long paragraphs with minimal breaks make it difficult to scan or pick out key points.
- **Weak connection to visuals:**  
  Text and graphics seem unrelated; the viewer has to guess which text goes with which image or chart.
- **Missing explanations:**  
  Data and facts are listed without clarifying what they mean or why they matter.
- **Inconsistent level and voice:**  
  The writing jumps between very technical language and casual language without clear purpose.

## Storytelling and Flow

- **No clear narrative:**  
  Facts are thrown together with no apparent order (e.g., a random mix of definitions, statistics, and images).
- **Confusing sequence:**  
  There is no clear “start” or “end,” and the viewer must decide their own path, often missing important pieces.
- **Abrupt or missing conclusion:**  
  The infographic ends without summarizing the main idea or takeaways.

## Creativity and Thematic Design

- **Cliché or copied design:**  
  Layout and visuals look like generic templates or obvious copies of existing infographics without adaptation.
- **Theme conflicts with readability:**  
  Overly decorative backgrounds, textures, or themed graphics make text and charts difficult to see.
- **Random decorative elements:**  
  Shapes, icons, or patterns appear with no purpose other than filling space.

## References and Attribution

- **Missing or incomplete references:**  
  Sources are not listed, or only vague references (“Google,” “Wikipedia”) appear.
- **Inconsistent formatting:**  
  Citation styles are mixed or unclear, making it hard to identify full source information.
- **No image credits:**  
  Graphics and photos are used without any attribution, or attributions are hidden or too small to read.

---

These descriptions are intended for use “behind the scenes” when giving feedback.  
The exemplar description should guide what strong design looks like; the poor description should help identify specific design weaknesses and suggest targeted improvements.
